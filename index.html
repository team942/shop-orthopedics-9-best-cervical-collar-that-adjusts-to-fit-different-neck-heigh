Finding a cervical collar that actually fits is harder than it looks. Neck height, chin prominence, shoulder width, and tracheostomy needs all vary enormously from patient to patient — and a collar that sits too tall pushes the chin into extension, while one that's too short fails to immobilize the spine. This guide covers the nine adjustable cervical collars worth considering in 2026, ranked by fit range, adjustability, comfort over long wear, and value.

What to Consider

Before you compare models, understand the five factors that determine whether a collar will work for your neck — and your recovery.

Adjustable height range. The single most important spec. A collar that offers tool-free height adjustment across a range of roughly 2 to 3 inches will cover far more neck lengths than a fixed-height shell. Two-piece designs with a front and back panel that telescope independently are the most versatile, because your anterior and posterior neck heights are rarely identical.

Size and circumference fit. Height gets the attention, but circumference matters just as much. Most manufacturers run X-Small through XL, with some offering bariatric and pediatric sizes. Measure your neck circumference at the widest point and match it to the manufacturer's chart rather than guessing from collar height alone.

Immobilization versus comfort. Rigid foam shells hold the spine still but can create pressure points at the chin, occiput, and clavicle. Look for collars with contoured chin pieces, breathable or replaceable padding, and a tracheostomy opening if you need airway access. If your clinician wants strict immobilization, prioritize rigidity first and comfort second.

Ease of application. You or a caregiver will be putting this on and taking it off several times a day. Quick-release buckles, hook-and-loop straps that adjust without rethreading, and a front panel that opens flat all reduce the friction — and the risk of a poorly seated collar.

Price and total cost of ownership. Collars range from about $40 to nearly $200. Factor in replacement pads, since foam liners compress and lose support over weeks of wear, and check whether the model is eligible for HSA/FSA reimbursement before you buy.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I measure my neck for a cervical collar?

Two numbers matter: neck height and neck circumference. For height, measure from the top of your shoulder near the base of the neck up to the bottom of your jawbone with your head in a neutral position — not tilted. For circumference, measure around the widest part of your neck. Write both down and compare against the manufacturer's chart rather than defaulting to a size based on your shirt collar.

Can one adjustable collar really fit different neck heights?

Yes, within limits. Two-piece designs with independent front and back panel adjustment typically cover a spread of two to three inches of neck height, which handles most adults. The fit breaks down at the extremes — very short or very long necks, and very large circumferences — which is why specialty and bariatric sizes exist. If you're near the edge of a size chart, size up rather than trying to force the smaller shell.

How often should I replace the padding?

Replace foam pads when they stop springing back, when the surface starts to crack or pill, or roughly every four to six weeks of continuous wear. Compressed padding is the most common reason a collar that fit well on day one starts to feel loose and unsupportive. Keep a spare set on hand if you're in a multi-week protocol.

Are cervical collars HSA/FSA eligible?

In most cases, yes — physician-prescribed cervical collars and replacement pads generally qualify as medical expenses. Many retailers accept HSA/FSA payment at checkout or provide an itemized receipt you can submit for reimbursement. Check the specific listing before you buy, and keep your prescription documentation.
